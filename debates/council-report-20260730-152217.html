Why Wispr over-rewrites long technical dictation

The cleanup stage isn't broken. It's obeying an instruction that contradicts the project's own doctrine — and it has never been benchmarked on the input that fails.

bluejay-wispr · 30 July 2026 · unanimous peer verdict: First Principles strongest, Expansionist biggest blind spot

The finding in one paragraph

CLAUDE.md says losing the user's words is the worst outcome. The system prompt says “rewrite disfluent speech into complete, coherent sentences… should read like text the speaker would have typed.” Those cannot both be true. The model is following rule 3 correctly; rule 3 is the wrong rule for a prompt destined for a coding agent, whose reader is another LLM that does not care about “um.” Every guard in the pipeline is an attempt to detect unwanted rewriting after the fact. On the paths that don't want it, make it structurally impossible instead.

Where the council agrees

High confidence

1. The prompt contradicts the doctrine

Four of five advisors found this independently, without being pointed at it. There is no bug to fix — the instruction is being followed. It is the instruction that's wrong, per context.

High confidence

2. The benchmark cannot see the reported failure

17 cases, none over ~170 words, none an AI-agent prompt, scored as a percentage by an LLM judge. The complaint is about 200–300 word technical dictation, which is untested. Verified worse than stated: bench.py defaults to “every loaded model” and the committed results cover only qwen3-0.6b and qwen3-1.7b — while LLMCleaner.firstModel(at:) leads its priority list with qwen3-4b-2507. The shipped model has never appeared in a committed run.

High confidence

3. The guards enforce length, not fidelity

looksTruncated catches collapse and inflation. It cannot catch the actual failure: a 1:1-length output that swapped content words or silently dropped one of three enumerated options. Under today's guards the doctrine is decorative.

High confidence

4. “Only where the fit is obvious” is not computable

Unanimous: constrain sound-alike substitution to a closed set (draftTerms ∪ vocabulary) rather than leaving it to a 4B model's judgment. The only disagreement is whether enforcement lives in the prompt or in Swift. Both, ideally.

Where the council clashes

Unresolved by the advisors

Is sound-alike substitution the best rule or the worst?

The Contrarian calls it a licence to rewrite correct words and wants it out of the prompt, reimplemented as phonetic matching in Swift. The Expansionist calls it the highest-value line and wants it expanded. Both are right about different halves: the goal is the highest value in the prompt — recognition is the measured bottleneck — and the mechanism is the worst, because it's unbounded model discretion. Resolution: keep the goal, close the set.

Unresolved by the advisors

LLM or no LLM on the coding path?

The Contrarian says skip the model entirely: ruleClean plus symbol expansion is ~40 lines and cannot mangle FLAG_RETRY_V2. First Principles and the Executor keep the model but shrink its job. The Contrarian's version is cheaper and strictly safe, but gives up the two things regex can't do: “dash dash verbose” → --verbose across arbitrary phrasings, and sentence boundaries in a 250-word run-on. Resolution: keep the model, constrain it structurally — and the Contrarian's version is the correct fallback when the invariant fails.

Settled on review

Is the KV-cache layout costing quality?

The Contrarian wants it deleted as premature optimization in a pipeline where “neither stage is the bottleneck.” The Executor showed this is a false tradeoff: two static prefixes selected by category keep the cache and put the category at the top of the prompt. The Executor is right; the cache costs nothing here. And with a sub-600ms budget it is now load-bearing.

Blind spots the peer review caught

Missed by all five advisors

The few-shots undercut any low-touch prompt you write

Every advisor rewrote prose rules and left the four demonstrations alone. In a 4B model, demonstrations beat instructions — a “change nothing else” rule paired with exemplars showing heavy rewriting loses. Two reviewers caught this independently.

Missed by all five advisors

In-context length bias is the mechanism behind a length-specific complaint

Verified: the longest demonstrated output in LLMCleaner.fewShot is the bug-report example, roughly 55 words. The failing inputs are 200–300 words — 4–6× the longest thing the model has ever been shown. It imitates demonstrated output length and condenses. This explains why the complaint is length-specific while every proposed fix is category-specific, and it puts chunking — which four advisors dismissed — back on the table as the mechanism-matched fix.

Missed by all five advisors

Three more, cheap

  • The 600-char draft sitting in the final user message immediately before the transcript invites imitation and continuation, whatever the label says. It may itself be causing rewriting.
  • temperature 0.2 should be 0 for a deterministic transform. bench.py currently scores every rep precisely because output isn't deterministic.
  • Nobody proposed the only real parity ruler: run the same ten dictations through Wispr Flow and diff. The stated goal is 1:1 parity with an unmeasured target.

Added after the council: the latency budget

Constraint the council never saw

A sub-500–600ms end-to-end target is not a nice-to-have here — it disqualifies most of the “bigger model” and “two-pass” options outright, and it re-ranks the recommendation. With the transcription tail measured at ~140ms, cleanup gets roughly 360–460ms.

ModelMedianWorstFits the budget?
qwen3-0.6b-mlx250ms1050msAt the median, yes. Tail breaks it.
qwen3-1.7b670ms5420msNo — over budget before the tail.
qwen3-4b-2507 (shipped default)~600msunmeasuredNo — and never benchmarked.

Two things follow. First, the shipped default is over budget and nobody has measured it. Second, and more useful: a hard deadline beats a model choice for the tail. Race the cleanup call against a timer — if it hasn't returned in ~400ms, emit ruleClean(raw) and move on. That converts every latency outlier into a fidelity-safe fallback, which is exactly what the doctrine asks for, and it makes a “fast” setting an honest promise rather than an average.

There's a happy convergence worth naming: the low-touch job is shorter output, which is fewer decode tokens, which is faster. Delete-and-repunctuate is a latency win and a fidelity win at the same time. The over-editing fix and the speed fix are the same fix.

The model-tier UI

Recommendation

Two options, not three. Three forces a judgment nobody has data for yet, and the middle one gets picked by default while meaning nothing. Two is also all you can currently make honest: the bench distinguishes 0.6b from 1.7b/4b, and nothing in between.

Word them as outcomes, not model ids — CLAUDE.md is explicit that endpoint names, model ids, and fallback states are not information a user acts on. Provider and model already live in Advanced, so that's where this belongs, as the one control for the job:

Cleanup ( ) Fast — cleans up in about a quarter second ( ) Careful — slower, better on long dictation

Do not ship this before the long bench cases exist. Right now “more accurate” is unsubstantiated — on the committed numbers the bigger model scores lower (0.6b at 90%, 1.7b at 83%), so a “slower and more accurate” label would be a claim the data contradicts. Build the ruler, find out which model actually wins on 200–300 word technical dictation, and let the labels say what's true. If it turns out the small model wins everywhere once the job is smaller, the right UI is no control at all — which would be the best outcome available and is entirely consistent with this project's UI doctrine.

The recommendation

Adopt First Principles' diagnosis with the Executor's diff, add the two mechanisms the reviewers surfaced, and let the latency budget kill the expensive options.

  1. Bench cases that contain the failure. 6–8 real 200–300 word dictations — agent prompts, design-doc rambles, enumerated options — hand-corrected into references. Run against qwen3-4b-2507 so the eval finally covers production. Measure: content-word recall (fraction of raw non-stopword tokens surviving) + a substitution count + p95 latency, not just median.
  2. Two static prefixes selected by category. if category == .coding picks a low-touch prefix; everything else keeps today's. Cache survives, category lands at the top of the prompt. Delete the self-correction rule and the “rewrite into coherent sentences” rule from the low-touch variant entirely. Measure: content-word recall on the new cases, before/after.
  3. The subsequence guard. Cleaned tokens must be a subsequence of raw tokens, modulo case, punctuation, and the spoken-symbol map. Fail → emit ruleClean(raw). One function replacing looksTruncated's ratio heuristics on the low-touch path — enforcing the doctrine instead of decorating it. Measure: how often it fires. Near-zero means the prompt is landing; a high rate means it isn't.
  4. A hard cleanup deadline. ~400ms, then fall back to ruleClean(raw). Makes the latency target a guarantee rather than an average, and reuses the fallback path you already trust. Measure: p95 end-to-end from key release to text at cursor; deadline hit rate.
  5. Long + coding few-shots. At least one demonstration whose output runs 200+ words, and one spoken AI-agent prompt with enumerated options preserved intact. No prompt wording fixes in-context length bias — only a longer demonstration does. Measure: whether recall on the long cases moves independently of change #2. Watch prefill cost — a long few-shot is cached, but it isn't free.
  6. Close the sound-alike set. Substitute only words present in the already-typed text or the vocabulary list, enforced by the same guard, whose allowed alphabet becomes raw ∪ draftTerms ∪ vocab ∪ punctuation. Measure: substitution count on cases containing correct-but-unusual words.
  7. Cheap wins, same afternoon. Capture the draft at recording END; temperature 0; split coding into terminal (short, symbol-heavy) and agent_prompt (long prose, zero rewriting). A Claude Code chat box and a shell prompt want opposite things and currently share a bucket.
  8. Then, and only then. Length-conditional chunking if recall still degrades with length; raise the 50-term contextualStrings cap and measure it (nobody has); vocabulary autolearning from HistoryStore edits — the Expansionist's one genuinely cheap idea. Note: chunking serializes on one local model server, so it trades directly against the latency budget. Measure both.

Explicitly rejected: the LoRA fine-tune (all five reviewers flagged it as the most expensive route to “stop rewriting my words,” and it would train against a ruler that can't see the failure); the “Agent Prompt” structuring mode (more rewriting, aimed at the context that wants less); deleting the KV-cache layout (a false tradeoff, and now load-bearing for latency); two-pass cleanup (doubles the call in a 400ms budget); and n-best rescoring as described — SpeechAnalyzer is the primary path, and the alternatives API the Expansionist assumed belongs to SFSpeechRecognizer. Verify before planning on it.

One correction to the framing that started this: “replicate Wispr Flow 1:1” is the wrong target if Flow is running a frontier cloud model. The winnable game is fidelity on technical dictation — being the tool that never mangles a prompt — not matching Flow's prose polish with a 4B local model inside 400ms.

The one thing to do first

Record 6–8 real 200–300 word dictations — the exact kind that fail, dictated normally into Claude Code and a terminal — hand-correct each into a reference, add them to bench/cases.json, and run the bench against qwen3-4b-2507 scoring content-word recall and p95 latency. You will learn in an afternoon whether the failure is the rewrite rule, the length bias, or the model — and whether the fast/careful UI has anything true to say. Every other change on this list becomes measurable instead of plausible.

Full advisor responses

The First Principles Thinker strongest, 5/5

You're asking how to tune a rewriter. The prior question is: who consumes this text? When he dictates a prompt for an AI coding agent, the consumer is another LLM that does not care about “um.” Cleanup there has ~zero upside and unbounded downside. You are running a lossy generative transform to serve a reader who doesn't exist.

So the root cause isn't the rewrite instruction, the self-correction rule, or model size. It's that one stage is doing two categorically different jobs: deletion (fillers, stutters, false starts, spoken-symbol mapping) and generation (re-wording into sentences the speaker didn't say). Deletion is verifiable and provably lossless-in-meaning. Generation is unverifiable, and no prompt sentence makes a 4B model reliably self-police it. All your guards — looksTruncated, edit-distance ideas, ellipsis bans — are attempts to detect generation after the fact. Stop detecting it. Make it structurally impossible.

Also note your prompt contradicts your own doctrine. Doctrine: losing words is the worst outcome. Prompt: “rewrite disfluent speech… should read like text the speaker would have typed.” Those cannot both be true. Pick, per context.

Ranked:

  1. Coding/AI-prompt category → delete-only mode. Same model, different prompt: remove fillers and abandoned restarts, add punctuation/capitalization, map spoken symbols. Change no other word. Then validate: cleaned tokens must be a subsequence of raw tokens (modulo punctuation/case/symbol map). Fail → emit raw. That guard is one function and replaces three heuristics.
  2. Ship raw-vs-cleaned undo (hotkey restores verbatim). This dissolves the aggressiveness question — you stop needing to be right.
  3. Then spend on recognition, which you measured as the bottleneck.
  4. Prompt-tuning the current rewriter: last. You're polishing the wrong stage.
The Executor best diff-level plan

Monday morning, in order. Each item is a diff you can ship the same day.

1. Write the 6 long AI-prompt bench cases first (2 hours). You have 17 cases and zero of the failure mode you're complaining about. Every change below is unmeasurable until this exists. Score by content-word recall, not vibes: fraction of raw content words present in output. That's the metric, not “quality 90%.”

2. Two system prompts, selected by category (30 lines of Swift). Not a runtime-configurable aggressiveness dial — one if category == .coding picking one of two static strings. Both prefixes stay cache-warm; you get category at the top of the prompt for free, fixing (e). The coding variant:

Cleanup rules (low-touch mode): Remove fillers, stutters, and immediately-repeated words. Add punctuation and capitalization. Fix only clearly-wrong word forms. Do NOT reorder words, do NOT merge or split sentences, do NOT rephrase. Every content word in the transcript appears in the output unless it is a filler or a stutter. When unsure, leave it alone.

Delete the self-correction rule and the “rewrite into coherent sentences” rule from this variant entirely. “Judge it by whether the speaker replaced it” is not a thing a 4B model does — it's an instruction you'd write for yourself.

3. Constrain the sound-alike rule to a closed set. “Only substitute a word that appears in the already-typed text or the user's vocabulary list.” Enforce it in Swift: reject any output token not in raw ∪ draftTerms ∪ vocab ∪ punctuation. That's your edit-distance guard and it's cheap.

4. Add two coding few-shots to the coding prefix: one spoken AI-agent prompt with enumerated options preserved, one with --force/.env.

5. Snapshot draft at recording END. One-line move of the ContextDetector call.

Skip chunking, two-pass, constrained decoding, bigger models. Re-run the bench after 1–4 before touching architecture.

The Contrarian sharpest on the eval

The complaint is unfalsifiable with your current eval, and that's the fatal flaw. 17 short cases, self-scored “90% quality,” and not one of them is a 300-word dictated agent prompt — the exact input he's complaining about. Every prompt tweak below this line is guesswork until the benchmark contains 10 real long technical dictations scored by content-word edit distance vs. his own hand-corrected reference. Fix the ruler before the thing being measured.

Second: your guards don't enforce your doctrine. looksTruncated catches length collapse only. A model that returns 1:1 word count while swapping “quarry”→“query”, “prod”→“proud”, and silently deleting one of three enumerated options passes every guard you have. That's precisely the reported failure. Add a content-word Jaccard/edit-distance guard — reject if >15% of non-stopword tokens changed — or admit the doctrine is decorative.

Third, the uncomfortable one: for coding, don't call the LLM. ruleClean + punctuation + symbol expansion is a ~40-line Swift function, 0ms, and cannot mangle FLAG_RETRY_V2. Ship that behind the category check, measure it against the 4B, and I suspect it wins on the cases he cares about. You are paying 300ms and all your fidelity risk for polish he explicitly doesn't want.

On (b): “Only where the fit is obvious” is not a computable instruction for a 4B model — it's a licence. Move sound-alike correction into Swift as a phonetic match restricted to draftTerms + custom vocabulary. Closed set, auditable, no drift.

On (e): you optimized 300ms in a pipeline where “neither is the bottleneck.” That KV layout is premature optimization that demonstrably degrades the coding path. Delete it.

And (g): Flow uses a frontier cloud model. 4B local won't match it. Pick fidelity, not parity.

The Outsider named the framing error

The framing itself is what confuses me. You call this a “cleanup stage” but the prompt says “rewrite disfluent speech into complete, coherent sentences… read like text the speaker would have typed.” That is not cleanup, that's ghostwriting. And your doctrine says losing the user's words is the worst outcome. The prompt and the doctrine say opposite things. The model isn't misbehaving — it's obeying rule 3. Everything downstream (guards, edit-distance ideas, two-pass) is compensating for an instruction you chose to give.

Second confusion: why does a prompt for a coding agent need to be a well-formed sentence at all? The recipient is a machine that reads disfluency better than a human does. Nobody is judging your grammar. So for that context the right output is: fillers gone, punctuation added, words untouched. That's the whole feature. You don't need a smarter model, you need a smaller job.

Third: coding as one bucket is the actual bug. A terminal (short, symbol-heavy, wants “dash dash force”) and a Claude Code chat box (long prose, wants zero rewriting) have opposite needs and share a category. Split them. agent_prompt gets: “Remove fillers and stutters. Add punctuation and capitalization. Change nothing else. Do not reorder, merge, or reword. Do not resolve self-corrections.”

Fourth, and biggest: your benchmark doesn't test the complaint. 17 cases, short ones, “90% quality” scored by whom? The owner's pain is long technical dictation and you have no long technical dictation case. Record ten real 200-word agent-prompt dictations, and score one number: fraction of the speaker's content words that survive. Optimize that. Right now you're tuning against a test that can't see the failure.

Sound-alike rule: keep it, but only for terms present in draftTerms or vocabulary. Unbounded, it's a licence.

The Expansionist biggest blind spot, 5/5

The undervalued asset is HistoryStore, and it's sitting there doing nothing.

Every dictation already stores raw + cleaned. Add “user edited the inserted text within 30s” capture and you own a paired corpus of his speech → his preferred text. That is the one thing Wispr Flow cannot have: personalization. Two payoffs, escalating:

  1. Autolearn vocabulary (ship this week). Any token the user typed that wasn't in the transcript, and any correction pair, goes into contextualStrings permanently. This fixes .env/FLAG_RETRY_V2 at the recognizer, where it belongs. Raise the cap to whatever SpeechAnalyzer tolerates — test 200, 500. Nobody measured 50; it's a guess.
  2. Fine-tune a 0.6b LoRA on 500 of his own pairs (this quarter). Then the prompt shrinks to nothing, the KV-cache debate evaporates, latency halves, and over-editing stops being a prompt-wrestling problem because the model has learned his register. A local dictation app with a self-training cleanup model is a category nobody occupies.

Expand the sound-alike rule, don't retreat from it. It's the highest-value line in the prompt and it's crippled by being a vibe instead of a mechanism. SFSpeechRecognizer gives you n-best alternatives — score them against draftTerms/open-file symbols and pick the winner deterministically. Recognition is the measured bottleneck; this is where the ceiling is.

Then go bigger than parity. Dictating prompts for coding agents is a fast-growing use with zero purpose-built tooling. Ship two modes on the same pipeline: Verbatim (delete-and-repunctuate only, edit-distance-gated) for code, and opt-in Agent Prompt that structures a ramble into goal/constraints/files. Users want less rewriting for code and more for specs — one setting, two products.

Also: capture the draft at END, add coding few-shots (free), and surface the guard as a visible “revert to raw” hotkey — that's trust, not plumbing.
